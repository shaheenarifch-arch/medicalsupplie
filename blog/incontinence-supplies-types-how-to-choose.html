Best Incontinence Supplies for Elderly: Types & How to Choose

Not sure which incontinence products to buy for an elderly parent? Compare briefs, pull-ups, pads, and bed protection, and learn how to choose the right fit.

Quick answer

For most elderly parents at home, start with the right disposable product for their mobility: pull-up underwear if they walk and toilet independently, or tabbed briefs if they need help changing. Add pads for light leaks, bed protection for nights, and gentle skin care. Adjust after a week of use.

Key takeaways

  • Match the product to mobility first: pull-ups for people who walk, tabbed briefs for people who need help or are mostly in bed or a chair.
  • Choose absorbency by leak volume and time of day, not by habit. Many people need a light product by day and a heavier one overnight.
  • A proper fit matters more than a bigger pad. Gaps at the legs cause most leaks, so measure the waist and hips.
  • Bed pads, chair pads, and no-rinse cleansers are part of the system, not extras.
  • Buy small packs of two or three styles to test before stocking up, and involve your parent's clinician if you see skin damage or a sudden change.

How to choose the right product

1. Check absorbency honestly

Packaging uses terms like light, moderate, heavy, and overnight, but brands define them differently. Instead of trusting the label alone, watch what happens in real life:

  • If the product is barely damp after several hours, you may be able to go lighter during the day.
  • If it leaks or feels saturated before the next scheduled change, move up in absorbency or switch styles.
  • If your parent wakes wet at night, use a heavier or overnight product at bedtime and add a bed pad underneath.

Heavier is not automatically better. Thicker products can be warmer and bulkier, and they may encourage longer wear than is good for the skin.

2. Get the size and fit right

Sizing is where many caregivers go wrong. Most brands size by waist and hip measurement, and the chart on the package is more reliable than guessing from clothing size. Measure around the fullest part of the hips, and use the larger measurement if the waist and hips fall into different sizes.

A good fit looks like this:

  • The leg openings sit snugly against the skin with no gaps, and the leg cuffs are tucked out and up, not folded in.
  • The waistband is snug but doesn't dig in or leave deep marks.
  • Tabs on a brief are fastened with the lower tabs angled up and the upper tabs angled down, so the product stays close to the body.

If your parent's size falls between two, try both. A brief that is too large gaps and leaks, and one that is too small can irritate the skin.

3. Think about who does the changing

An independent parent usually prefers pull-ups because they feel more dignified and are quick to change in the bathroom. If you're changing your parent in bed, tabbed briefs are often easier because you can open them at the sides without lifting your parent's legs through leg holes. Look for refastenable tabs, which let you check and adjust the fit.

4. Consider skin and sensitivity

Look for breathable outer layers, a wetness indicator if your parent can't tell when they are wet, and unscented products if the skin is easily irritated. A parent who has dementia or limited sensation may not report discomfort, so you'll want to check the skin during changes.

Best picks by situation

Active parent, light leaks

Start with liners or light pads in regular, snug underwear. If leaks still get through, try a moderate pad or pull-up underwear. Keep a spare product in a bag or purse for outings.

Walks with some help, moderate to heavy leaks

Pull-up underwear is usually the best starting point. If standing to change is difficult or unsafe, consider tabbed briefs instead, even if your parent is still mobile for part of the day.

Mostly in bed or a chair, needs full assistance

Use tabbed briefs with an underpad on the bed or chair. Keep supplies near where you change your parent so you aren't leaving them exposed while you search for items. Many caregivers stock a small basket with briefs, wipes, gloves, barrier cream, and disposal bags.

Mainly nighttime leaks

Use a day product that is comfortable and a higher-absorbency product at night. Also consider limiting how much your parent drinks right before bed only if their doctor agrees. Hydration matters, especially for older adults.

Bowel incontinence

Briefs with good side containment generally work better than pads. Change promptly, since stool irritates skin quickly. Ask the clinician about causes and treatments, because constipation, medication effects, and other treatable factors can play a role.

Bed, chair, and skin protection

Even the best brief can leak sometimes, so protect the surfaces your parent uses most. Disposable underpads work well on beds and in recliners. Washable pads are a good option if you'd rather do more laundry than buy more disposables, and some are made for wheelchairs. A waterproof mattress cover underneath adds another layer.

Skin care is just as important as absorbency. Moisture, urine, and stool can break down fragile older skin. A simple routine helps:

  1. Change soiled products promptly rather than waiting for a set schedule.
  2. Cleanse gently with a no-rinse cleanser or soft wipes, and pat dry rather than rub.
  3. Apply a barrier cream or ointment as directed on the label.
  4. Check the skin at every change for redness, open areas, or pain.

If you notice redness that doesn't fade, blisters, or broken skin, contact your parent's healthcare provider. Dressings and skin protectants may be needed, and our guide to wound care supplies online explains what to look for when buying them.

Common buying mistakes to avoid

  • Buying a big case first. Brands fit differently. Buy a small pack of two or three options and compare leaks, comfort, and ease of changing.
  • Choosing by absorbency alone. A heavy-duty brief that doesn't fit will leak. Fit comes first.
  • Using pads inside pull-ups or briefs without a reason. Doubling up can add bulk and block the product's built-in absorbent layers. If one product isn't enough, a different product is usually better than stacking.
  • Skipping skin care. Supplies that hold urine are only half the job; the other half is keeping the skin clean and dry.
  • Ignoring your parent's preferences. Incontinence is embarrassing for many older adults. Let your parent help choose, use neutral language, and keep changes private.
  • Forgetting disposal. Stock disposal bags and a lidded bin to help control odor. Don't flush briefs or pads.

When to involve a clinician

Incontinence is common, but it is not something to simply accept without a checkup. A sudden change, burning with urination, new confusion, or a large increase in leaks can point to a treatable issue such as an infection or medication side effect. A clinician can also tell you whether products like external catheters, or strategies like scheduled toileting, are appropriate. Coverage for supplies varies by insurance plan and state program, so it's worth asking your parent's plan or local agency what, if anything, they cover.

Bottom line

The best incontinence supplies for an elderly parent depend on mobility, leak volume, and skin health. Pull-ups suit people who walk, tabbed briefs suit people who need help, and pads and liners handle lighter leaks. Add bed protection and good skin care, test small packs before committing, and check in with a clinician if anything changes suddenly.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I change an incontinence brief or pull-up?

Change it as soon as possible after a bowel movement and whenever it is noticeably wet or full. There isn't one schedule for everyone, so check regularly, especially if your parent can't tell you when they are wet. Prompt changes help protect the skin.

Can I use a pad inside a pull-up or brief for extra protection?

It's usually better to switch to a more absorbent product than to stack layers. Doubling up can add bulk, cause gaps at the legs, and block the product's built-in absorbent design. If leaks keep happening, recheck the size and fit first.

What if my parent is between two sizes?

Use the manufacturer's size chart and measure around the hips, using the larger measurement if the waist and hips differ. If it's still unclear, buy a small pack of each size. The right one fits snugly at the legs and waist without digging in.

Are washable incontinence products worth it?

They can work well for light to moderate leaks if you are comfortable doing extra laundry. For heavy leaks, nights, or bowel incontinence, disposable products usually hold more. Some families use washable items by day and disposables at night.

How can I reduce odor from used incontinence products?

Change products promptly, clean the skin at every change, and bag used items before putting them in a lidded trash bin. Some products have odor-control features. If a strong odor persists, ask a clinician, since it can signal an infection or skin problem.

How do I talk to my parent about starting to use incontinence products?

Choose a private moment, stay calm and matter-of-fact, and focus on comfort and independence. Let your parent help pick the product and style, since pull-ups often feel more familiar than briefs. Reassure them that this is common and manageable.
