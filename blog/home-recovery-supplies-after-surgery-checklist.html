How to Set Up a Home Recovery Station After Surgery: Supplies Checklist

A practical checklist of home recovery supplies after surgery: what to keep at your bedside, how to set up a safe space before your operation, and when to call your surgeon.

By Shaheen Arif, Founder and Editor · Reviewed by Talha N. Ch · Not reviewed by a medical professionalUpdated November 19, 202614 min read

Coming home after surgery is a relief, but the first few days can feel harder than expected. Pain medicine makes you drowsy, getting out of bed takes planning, and the dressing on your incision suddenly becomes your job. A home recovery station, one well-stocked spot where everything you need is within arm's reach, takes much of that stress away. This guide explains what to put in it, where to set it up and which warning signs mean you should call your surgeon.

Quick answer

Most people need a bedside station with their medicines and a pill organizer, a thermometer, the dressing supplies their surgical team names, disposable gloves, cold packs if approved, a water bottle, a phone charger and a notebook, plus fall-prevention basics like a nightlight, clear paths and supportive closed-back shoes. Your discharge instructions decide the exact list.

How to use this checklist: Read it one to two weeks before your surgery date, then compare it with the written instructions from your surgeon's office. Tick off what you already own, cross out anything your team says you won't need, and shop for the rest early. If your instructions and this article ever disagree, follow your instructions.

Important: This is general planning information, not medical advice. Your surgeon's discharge instructions always come first, and you should check with your care team before using any product on or near an incision.

What is a home recovery station, and why set one up before surgery?

A home recovery station is a planned area, usually around a bed or recliner, where you'll spend most of your time in the first days or weeks after an operation. Instead of asking someone to fetch things every few minutes, or getting up before you're steady, you keep the essentials organized and close by.

Setting it up before surgery matters for three reasons:

  • You won't feel like shopping afterwards. Anesthesia, pain medicine and limited movement make store trips hard, and online orders can take days.
  • Fewer trips mean fewer falls. Many people feel dizzy or weak the first few times they stand after surgery.
  • Caregivers can help more easily. When supplies are labeled and in one place, anyone can step in without guessing.

If you're building a general stock of supplies for an older relative, our complete guide to home medical supplies covers the wider picture. This post focuses on the short, intense period right after you come home.

Where should you set up a recovery station?

Choose a room close to a bathroom

You'll make many bathroom trips, and a short, straight walk is safer than a long hallway. If your bedroom is upstairs and your team has told you to limit stairs at first, consider setting up on the main floor for the first week or two.

Bed, recliner or both?

Some people rest comfortably in bed. Others, for example after certain shoulder or abdominal operations, find a recliner easier to get in and out of, or are told to sleep more upright. Ask your surgeon's office which position they recommend. A firm chair with armrests is usually easier to rise from than a deep sofa.

Clear the path and create a reach zone

Walk the route from your bed or chair to the bathroom and kitchen. Remove loose rugs, tuck away cords, keep pets' bowls and toys out of the way and leave enough width for a walker. Add plug-in nightlights for nighttime trips. Then place a sturdy side table or rolling cart on the side of the bed you'll get out of, so everything you use several times a day is within reach without twisting, bending low or stretching across your body.

Bedside essentials and medication management

After surgery you may be taking several new medicines on top of your usual ones, sometimes at different intervals, and mix-ups are easy when you're sleepy. A few simple tools help:

  • A pill organizer with morning, midday, evening and bedtime compartments. Ask your pharmacist whether all your medicines can safely go in one, as some need to stay in their original packaging.
  • A written schedule taped near the bed showing each medicine, dose and time, with space to note when each dose was actually taken.
  • A phone alarm for each dose, especially overnight.

Don't add over-the-counter pain relievers, sleep aids or supplements without checking with your surgeon or pharmacist first, because some can interact with prescribed medicines or affect bleeding.

Round out the bedside table with a water bottle with a straw or spout lid, snacks your diet instructions allow, tissues, lip balm, a small trash bag, reading glasses, headphones and a charging cable long enough to reach the bed.

Wound and incision care supplies

This is where your surgical team's instructions matter most. Incisions are closed and protected in different ways: stitches, staples, skin glue, adhesive strips or a special dressing that should stay on for a set number of days. Some should be kept dry; others can be gently washed. Never assume.

What to have ready (if your team says you'll need it)

  • The exact dressings your surgeon recommends. Ask for the type and size before you leave, or photograph the dressing that was used. Our guide to buying wound care supplies online explains the main dressing types.
  • Sterile gauze pads and medical tape for covering or padding, if instructed.
  • Disposable nitrile gloves. Wash your hands first, then glove up for dressing changes or personal care. Nitrile contains no natural rubber latex, so it suits latex-sensitive households. A case like the Finitex blue nitrile exam gloves keeps a caregiver stocked for weeks; our glove buying guide explains sizes and thickness.
  • Small clean scissors reserved for tape and gauze, and a covered tray or zip bag to keep wound supplies separate.
  • A mirror or phone camera to check incisions you can't see easily. A daily photo helps you and your nurse notice changes.

What not to do without permission

Don't apply creams, ointments, powders, hydrogen peroxide or alcohol to an incision unless your team tells you to. Don't soak the wound in a bath, hot tub or pool until you've been cleared, and don't remove adhesive strips or skin glue early. Many items here overlap with a household first aid kit, so check what you already have with our home first aid kit checklist before buying duplicates.

Comfort, swelling and rest

Cold therapy. Surgical teams often suggest cold for swelling and soreness, particularly after joint procedures. Reusable gel packs or a cold therapy wrap both work. Always put a thin cloth between the pack and your skin, follow the timing your team gives you, and take extra care if the area feels numb, because you may not notice skin getting too cold.

Pillows for positioning. You may be told to raise a leg or arm above heart level, or to sleep partly upright. Firm pillows or a foam wedge make this easier. After abdominal or chest surgery, nurses often show patients how to hold a pillow against the body when coughing or standing up, so keep one handy if that's part of your instructions.

Clothing and bedding. Front-opening shirts and loose, elastic-waist pants are easier around a sling, brace, drain or tender incision. A waterproof mattress protector or disposable bed pads can save stress at night.

Mobility aids and fall prevention

Falls are one of the biggest practical risks in the first days at home. You may be weaker than you feel, lightheaded from medicines, or new to a walker or crutches.

Walking aids. Use the exact device and height your physical therapist set, and practice with it before discharge if you can. If you're shopping for an older family member, our guide to mobility aids for seniors compares canes, walkers, rollators and wheelchairs and explains how to check the fit.

Footwear. Socks on a hard floor and backless slippers are common causes of slips. Choose shoes or slippers with a closed back, a secure fit and a sole with grip, that you can put on without bending far. Once your surgeon or therapist clears you for regular walks, a supportive lace-up walking shoe is a practical choice. WalkHero sells men's arch support shoes and women's arch support shoes; check the size chart and whether the style suits any swelling you still have.

Around the house. Move everyday kitchen items to counter height, and consider temporary grab bars or a toilet safety frame. Don't lean on towel racks for support; they aren't built to hold body weight.

Bathroom and hygiene supplies

The bathroom combines hard surfaces, water and bending, so many recovery plans include equipment here:

  • Raised toilet seat or toilet safety frame, often suggested after hip and knee surgery.
  • Shower chair or tub transfer bench, so you can sit while washing.
  • Handheld showerhead to keep water away from a dressing, and a non-slip bath mat.
  • Waterproof dressing covers, only if your team approves showering with one.

Ask when you may shower, whether the incision can get wet and how to dry it. Until then, sponge baths at the sink may be safer.

Some people notice temporary bladder changes after anesthesia or a urinary catheter, and nighttime trips can be hard while mobility is limited. Disposable bed pads or protective underwear can bridge the gap; our guide to incontinence supplies and how to choose them explains the options. Always report new burning or difficulty urinating to your care team.

Monitoring and record keeping

A digital thermometer and a plain notebook are enough for most people. Log medicine times, temperature readings, pain levels, walking, fluids, bowel movements and anything unusual about the wound. Only track blood pressure, blood sugar or oxygen levels if your team asks you to. Keep discharge papers, your medication list, follow-up details and the after-hours number in one folder by the bed.

Infection control for caregivers and visitors

Clean hands are one of the best ways to protect a healing incision. Put hand sanitizer at the bedside and near the front door, and ask anyone helping with wound care to wash with soap and water first. Keep visitors to a minimum for the first few days, and ask anyone with a cold, cough or stomach bug to postpone.

If a caregiver in the home gets sick but still needs to help, a well-fitting mask can reduce the spread of respiratory droplets during close contact. Masks such as the FuturePPE KN95 5-layer masks from Brookwood Med are one option to keep in the station; fit matters more than the label, so choose a size that seals around the nose and chin.

How the list changes by type of surgery

These examples show how priorities shift; they're not a substitute for your surgeon's guidance.

Hip or knee replacement

Expect more mobility equipment: a walker, raised toilet seat, shower chair, reacher and sock aid, plus cold therapy and leg elevation. Set up the station to avoid low chairs and deep bending if you have movement precautions.

Abdominal surgery

Lifting limits are common, so keep everything at waist height. A firm pillow for support when you cough, loose waistbands and approved easy-to-digest snacks help. Ask about constipation, which can happen with pain medicines and less activity, and what you're allowed to take for it.

Shoulder or arm surgery

You may be in a sling and using one hand. Choose front-opening tops, slip-on closed-back shoes, pump-style soap and pre-cut food, and ask whether a recliner is recommended for sleeping.

Minor or outpatient procedures

The station can be smaller: medicines, dressing supplies, a thermometer, water, a charger and a clear path to the bathroom. Have someone stay with you the first night if your team advised it after anesthesia.

A simple setup timeline

  1. Two weeks before: Ask your surgeon's office what supplies and equipment you'll need, and whether any will be provided or prescribed. Some equipment may be covered by insurance when prescribed, so check before you buy.
  2. One week before: Order supplies, choose the room, clear walking paths and fit nightlights. Arrange who will drive you home and stay with you.
  3. Two or three days before: Assemble the bedside station, charge devices and prepare easy meals. Practice getting in and out of bed or the recliner.
  4. Discharge day: Read the written instructions with your caregiver, ask about anything unclear and fill new prescriptions on the way home.
  5. First week home: Restock what runs out fastest, usually gloves, dressings and snacks, and move anything that's awkward to reach.

When should you call your doctor after surgery?

Your discharge paperwork should list the specific signs your surgical team wants to hear about. Keep it next to the bed and read it with your caregiver. As general guidance, these warning signs commonly need prompt attention:

Call your surgeon or care team promptly if you notice:

  • A fever or chills, especially above the temperature your instructions tell you to report.
  • Redness, warmth or swelling around the incision that is spreading or getting worse.
  • Pus, cloudy or bad-smelling discharge, or bleeding that soaks through the dressing.
  • The wound edges opening, or stitches or staples coming loose.
  • Pain, swelling, warmth or redness in a calf or leg, which can be a sign of a blood clot.
  • Pain that keeps getting worse or isn't controlled by your prescribed medicines.
  • Vomiting that stops you keeping fluids down, or trouble urinating.

Call 911 right away for trouble breathing, shortness of breath, chest pain, coughing up blood, fainting, confusion or heavy bleeding that won't stop.

If you're unsure whether something is normal, call. Surgical offices expect these calls, and it's always better to ask early.

Bottom line

A good recovery station doesn't need to be expensive. Start with your discharge instructions, then gather your medicines and a way to track them, the dressing supplies your team names, gloves and hand sanitizer, a thermometer, comfort items within reach, and the mobility and bathroom aids that keep you steady. Set it up before surgery, keep the warning-sign list where everyone can see it, and call your care team whenever something doesn't seem right.

Frequently asked questions

What medical supplies do I need at home after surgery?

Most people need their prescribed medicines and a pill organizer, a thermometer, the dressing supplies their surgical team names, disposable gloves, hand sanitizer, cold packs if approved, pillows for positioning, a water bottle, a phone charger and fall-prevention basics. Your discharge instructions decide the exact list.

When should I set up my recovery station?

One to two weeks before surgery is ideal. That leaves time to ask your surgeon's office what you'll need, order supplies, clear walking paths and practice getting in and out of bed or a recliner before you come home.

Do I need to buy wound dressings before surgery?

Ask first. Many surgical teams send you home with a specific dressing or tell you exactly what to buy, and some incisions need no dressing changes at all. Buying the wrong type wastes money, so wait for their instructions or ask for the product type and size in advance.

Should I use gloves when changing a surgical dressing at home?

Wash your hands first and follow your team's instructions. Disposable nitrile gloves are a common choice for caregivers doing dressing changes or personal care, and they're latex-free for people with a latex allergy.

Is a recliner or a bed better after surgery?

It depends on the operation. Some people rest well in bed, while others, for example after certain shoulder or abdominal procedures, find a recliner easier to get in and out of or are told to sleep more upright. Ask your surgeon which position they recommend.

What are warning signs after surgery that mean I should call my doctor?

Call your care team promptly for fever or chills, spreading redness or warmth around the incision, pus or bad-smelling discharge, bleeding through the dressing, the wound opening, calf pain or swelling, or pain that keeps getting worse. Call 911 for trouble breathing, chest pain or heavy bleeding.

Will insurance pay for equipment like walkers or shower chairs?

Sometimes. Some durable medical equipment may be covered when your doctor prescribes it, while other items are not. Check with your insurer and your surgeon's office before buying, and keep receipts and prescriptions.
